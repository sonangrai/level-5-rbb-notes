This note covers syllabus items **2.3 Credit-Deposit (CD) Ratio**, **2.4 Cash
Reserve Ratio (CRR)** and **2.6 Base Rate**. NRB uses all three to control
**how much banks lend** and **at what price**:

- **CD ratio**: how much of its deposits a bank may lend out.
- **CRR**: how much of its deposits a bank must keep idle with NRB.
- **Base rate**: the lowest interest rate a bank may charge on a loan.

## Credit-deposit (CD) ratio

The **CD ratio** shows how much of a bank's deposits it has lent out:

> **CD ratio = Total loans and advances ÷ Total deposits × 100**

- A **high** CD ratio means the bank has lent out most of its deposits. Its
  liquidity is **tight** and it has little room to lend more.
- A **low** CD ratio means a lot of deposits are sitting **idle**. The bank has
  **excess liquidity** and weak lending.

### NRB's limit

- **Maximum: 90%** for commercial banks, development banks and finance
  companies.
- Before **2022/23**, NRB used the **CCD ratio** (credit to **core capital plus
  deposits**). The **Monetary Policy 2079/80** replaced it with the CD ratio.
- NRB has since allowed some extra funding sources, such as certain borrowings
  and debentures, to count alongside deposits. The exact list changes with each
  year's monetary policy.

### Why it matters

- It stops banks from **lending beyond their deposits** and running into a
  liquidity crisis, as happened during Nepal's **credit crunches** of 2017 and
  2021/22.
- It links **credit growth** to **deposit growth**.

## Cash reserve ratio (CRR)

The **CRR** is the share of a bank's **deposits** that it must keep as **cash
balance with NRB**. The bank **earns no interest** on this money and cannot
lend it.

> **CRR = Balance held with NRB ÷ Total deposits × 100**

| Institution | CRR (recent monetary policies) |
| --- | --- |
| Commercial banks | **4%** |
| Development banks | **4%** |
| Finance companies | **3%** |

### How NRB uses it

- **Raising** the CRR takes money out of the banking system. Lending slows and
  inflation pressure falls.
- **Lowering** the CRR releases money for banks to lend. This **eases**
  liquidity.
- It also ensures that banks always have **cash available** to pay depositors.

### CRR compared with SLR

The **statutory liquidity ratio (SLR)** is set alongside the CRR. It requires
banks to hold a share of deposits in **liquid assets**: cash in the vault,
balances with NRB and **government securities**.

| Point | CRR | SLR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Held as | **Cash with NRB** only | Cash, NRB balance **and government securities** |
| Earns interest? | **No** | Yes, on the securities |
| Recent level, commercial banks | **4%** | **12%** |
| Purpose | Monetary control | Liquidity and solvency, plus funding government borrowing |

## Base rate

The **base rate** is the **lowest interest rate** a bank may charge on a loan.
Each bank works out its own base rate from **its own costs**, using NRB's
method. Every loan is priced as:

> **Lending rate = Base rate + Premium**

The **premium** depends on the borrower's risk, the type of loan and the
collateral.

NRB introduced the base rate in **2069/70 (2012/13)**. Before that, banks set
lending rates however they liked, and the pricing was not transparent.

### How it is calculated

The base rate adds up the bank's costs:

1. **Cost of funds**: the interest the bank pays on deposits and borrowings.
2. **Cost of CRR**: the income lost on deposits kept idle with NRB.
3. **Cost of SLR**: the income lost by holding low-yield liquid assets.
4. **Operating cost**: staff, premises, technology and similar expenses.

### Rules

- Banks **calculate and publish** their base rate **every month**.
- Lending below the base rate is generally **not allowed**. NRB sets specific
  exceptions, such as loans to staff and some priority-sector lending.
- The **premium** on an existing loan **cannot be raised** during the loan's
  life without the borrower's consent. The lending rate changes only when the
  **base rate** changes.
- The **interest-rate spread**, the gap between the average lending rate and
  the average deposit rate, is **capped by NRB**. For commercial banks the cap
  is about **4%**.

### Why it matters

- Loan pricing becomes **transparent**: borrowers can see what the bank's
  costs are and what it adds on top.
- When NRB changes policy, the effect reaches lending rates through each
  bank's costs.
- Banks cannot **undercut** each other on price and take on loans that do not
  cover their costs.

## Quick revision

> [!TIP]
> **One-line answers.** CD ratio = loans ÷ deposits, **maximum 90%**; it
> replaced the **CCD ratio** in **2079/80**. CRR = cash held with NRB ÷
> deposits; recent rates **4% / 4% / 3%** (commercial / development /
> finance); it **earns no interest**. SLR for commercial banks: **12%**. Base
> rate: introduced **2069/70**, published **monthly**, made up of **cost of
> funds + cost of CRR + cost of SLR + operating cost**; lending rate = base
> rate + premium.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Traps that show up in multiple-choice questions: **raising** the CRR
> **tightens** liquidity; **government securities count for SLR, not CRR**; the
> CRR balance **earns no interest**; the premium cannot rise **without the
> borrower's consent**; the **CCD ratio** (core capital plus deposits) is the
> **old** measure.

> [!WARNING]
> NRB revises the CRR, SLR, CD-ratio limit and spread cap in each year's
> **Monetary Policy**. Check the current year's policy before the exam.

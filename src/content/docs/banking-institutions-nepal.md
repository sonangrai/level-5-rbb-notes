A **financial institution** collects money from people who have more than they
need right now (savers) and lends it to people who need it now (borrowers).
This job is called **financial intermediation**. Nepal's financial system has
two parts:

- **Banking institutions**, which take deposits and are licensed and
  supervised by **Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB)**.
- **Non-bank financial institutions**, such as insurance companies and
  contractual savings funds, which have their own laws and regulators. See
  [Non-bank Financial Institutions](/docs/non-bank-financial-institutions-nepal).

## Legal framework

| Law | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058 (2002)** | Makes NRB the central bank and the regulator and supervisor of banks and financial institutions (BFIs) |
| **Bank and Financial Institution Act (BAFIA), 2073 (2017)** | Governs how BFIs are licensed, owned, managed and dissolved, and sorts them into classes A–D |
| **NRB Unified Directives** | Updated every year; set the prudential rules on capital, liquidity, loan classification, single-borrower limits, governance and other areas |

## Classification of BFIs

Under BAFIA, NRB licenses BFIs in **four classes**, plus a separate licence for
infrastructure lending.

| Class | Type | Main focus | Minimum paid-up capital (national level) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Commercial banks | Full range of banking services | **Rs 8 billion** |
| **B** | Development banks | SMEs, agriculture, industry | **Rs 2.5 billion** |
| **C** | Finance companies | Hire purchase, housing, consumer loans | **Rs 800 million** |
| **D** | Microfinance institutions | Low-income and deprived people | Set separately by NRB |
| — | Infrastructure development bank | Large, long-term infrastructure projects | **Rs 20 billion** |

> [!NOTE]
> NRB raised these capital requirements in **2015/16 (2072/73)**. Commercial
> banks went from Rs 2 billion to **Rs 8 billion**. Most BFIs could not raise
> that much capital on their own, so a wave of **mergers and acquisitions**
> followed, guided by NRB's merger and acquisition bylaws. The number of BFIs
> has been falling ever since.

## Commercial banks (Class A)

Commercial banks are the **largest and most important** part of Nepal's
financial system. They hold the **large majority of the system's deposits and
loans**.

### Role

- **Collecting deposits** through current, savings, call and fixed accounts.
- **Lending** to businesses, trade, industry, households and government.
- **Creating credit**: each loan becomes a new deposit somewhere, so lending
  expands the money supply.
- **Payments and remittance**: cheques, cards, mobile and internet banking,
  and inward remittance from Nepalis working abroad.
- **Foreign exchange and trade finance**: letters of credit (LCs), bank
  guarantees and currency exchange.
- **Carrying out monetary policy**: NRB's policy rates, the cash reserve ratio
  (CRR) and the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR) reach the economy through
  commercial banks.
- **Government business**: buying treasury bills and development bonds, and
  collecting revenue on the government's behalf.

### Contribution

- **Financial inclusion**: under NRB's **"one local level, one bank branch"**
  campaign, every one of the **753 local levels** now has at least one
  commercial bank branch.
- **Directed lending**: NRB requires minimum lending shares for **agriculture,
  energy, and micro, cottage and small enterprises (MSMEs)**. This sends credit
  to sectors the government has made a priority.
- **Digital payments**: banks run mobile banking, QR payments, ATMs and
  connectIPS, and so move transactions away from cash.
- **Jobs and revenue**: banks are among the largest formal employers and
  taxpayers in Nepal.

### Status

- There are **20 commercial banks**: the state-owned banks (including
  **Rastriya Banijya Bank**, **Nepal Bank** and **Agricultural Development
  Bank**), joint-venture banks and other private banks.
- Mergers since 2016 cut the number from about 30 to 20.
- Recent issues include **rising non-performing loans (NPLs)** since 2022/23,
  **swings between liquidity shortages and surpluses**, weak credit demand and
  pressure on interest-rate spreads.

## Development banks (Class B)

Development banks give **medium- and long-term credit** for productive sectors.
Their main customers are **small and medium enterprises, agriculture, tourism
and industry**.

### Role and contribution

- **Fund projects** in regions and sectors that commercial banks serve less.
- Give **project and term loans** for small industries and agro-businesses.
- **Mobilise deposits** from semi-urban and rural areas.
- Help **spread entrepreneurship** outside the main cities.

### Status

- There are **17 development banks**, down from about 90 in 2010/11 after
  mergers, upgrades and licence cancellations.
- Some of them still cover only a **province**, not the whole country.
- They face strong competition from commercial banks, which now have branches
  everywhere.

## Finance companies (Class C)

Finance companies are smaller deposit-taking institutions. They concentrate on
**consumer and retail lending**.

### Role and contribution

- **Hire purchase financing**, especially loans for vehicles and equipment.
- **Housing, real estate and personal loans.**
- **Fixed-deposit schemes**, often at higher interest rates than banks offer.
- **Credit for small traders** and self-employed people.

### Status

- There are **17 finance companies**. In 2010/11 there were about 80.
- The sector suffered **several failures and NRB takeovers** after the
  **real-estate bubble burst around 2010/11**, which damaged public trust.
- Many finance companies have since **merged into banks** or with each other.

## Microfinance institutions (Class D)

Microfinance institutions (*laghubitta*) offer **small, collateral-free loans**
to **low-income people**, mostly women. Most of them follow the **Grameen
group-lending model** that Bangladesh made famous.

### Role

- **Small loans without collateral**: borrowers form groups and guarantee each
  other's loans.
- **Compulsory and voluntary savings**, plus **micro-insurance** and
  **remittance** services.
- Training in **financial literacy and income-generating skills**.

### Contribution

- They bring finance to **remote and poor households** that banks do not reach.
- They help **women** gain economic independence.
- They support **self-employment** in livestock, small trade and farming.

### Status

- There are about **50 microfinance institutions**. Mergers have brought the
  number down from more than 90.
- **Over-indebtedness** has become a serious problem: one borrower often holds
  loans from several institutions. This led to **borrower protests in
  2023/24**, so NRB **capped interest rates**, **limited lending to one
  borrower** and **pushed for credit-information sharing**.
- Asset quality is weakening and NPLs are rising.

## Infrastructure development bank

The **Nepal Infrastructure Bank Ltd. (NIFRA)** is Nepal's only infrastructure
development bank. NRB licensed it in **2019 (2075/76)** under a separate
provision of BAFIA.

- Required **minimum paid-up capital: Rs 20 billion**, the highest for any type
  of BFI.
- Gives **long-term financing** for **hydropower, roads, airports, urban
  infrastructure and other large projects**.
- **Does not take ordinary public deposits.** It raises funds through capital,
  bonds and borrowing.
- It exists because commercial banks fund themselves with short-term deposits
  and so **cannot safely lend for 15–25 years**.

## Quick revision

> [!TIP]
> **One-line answers.** NRB regulates BFIs under the **NRB Act 2058** and
> **BAFIA 2073**. Classes: **A** commercial, **B** development, **C** finance,
> **D** microfinance. Minimum paid-up capital: **Rs 8 bn / 2.5 bn / 800 m**
> (national level). Infrastructure bank: **Rs 20 bn** (NIFRA, 2019).
> **"One local level, one bank branch"** covers all **753** local levels.
> Microfinance uses the **Grameen** group-lending model.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Traps that show up in multiple-choice questions: the **infrastructure bank
> does not take public deposits**; **development banks** focus on **SMEs and
> productive sectors**, not consumer lending; **finance companies** are known
> for **hire purchase**; the big **merger drive** started with the **2015/16
> capital increase**; BAFIA came in **2073**, replacing the
> **Bank and Financial Institutions Act, 2063**.
